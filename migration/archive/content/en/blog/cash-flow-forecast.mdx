# Cash Flow Forecast: The Startup's Essential Financial Tool

Cash flow is king. While startups focus on growth and revenue, many fail because they run out of cash. A robust cash flow forecast is the difference between scaling successfully and hitting the wall.

This comprehensive guide shows you how to build and maintain a 13-week rolling cash flow forecast—the gold standard in startup financial management.

## Why Cash Flow Forecasting Is Non-Negotiable for Startups

You can have positive earnings and still go bankrupt. Why? Because cash is different from profit.

**Real-world scenario:**
A SaaS startup grows revenue 50% year-over-year. Profit margins improve. Yet the CFO becomes increasingly anxious—because customer payments arrive 45 days late, while employee salaries must be paid weekly.

Without a cash flow forecast, you're flying blind.

### The Four Problems Cash Flow Forecasting Solves

1. **Anticipates Cash Gaps** - Identify months when you'll need external funding or when cash drops below safe minimums
2. **Optimizes Working Capital** - Understand the gap between paying suppliers and collecting from customers
3. **Secures Funding Conversations** - Investors want proof you've thought about cash runway; a good forecast is credible evidence
4. **Guides Decision-Making** - Knowing cash runway shapes decisions: hire now or wait? Launch new product or refocus?

## What Is a Cash Flow Forecast?

A cash flow forecast predicts every dollar flowing in and out of your business over a specific period—typically the next 13 weeks.

### Components of a Basic Cash Flow Forecast

**Inflows:**
- Customer payments (ARR, subscription, project fees)
- Investor funding rounds
- Loans or credit lines
- Other revenue (partnerships, grants)

**Outflows:**
- Employee salaries and benefits
- Vendor payments (COGS, subscriptions, tools)
- Rent and facilities
- Marketing and sales
- Professional services
- Taxes and compliance
- Debt repayment
- Capital expenditure (equipment, infrastructure)

**Key Formula:**
Beginning Cash + Total Inflows - Total Outflows = Ending Cash

## Why 13 Weeks? The Power of Rolling Forecasts

The 13-week rolling forecast is the Goldilocks solution:
- **Too short (1-4 weeks):** Misses seasonal patterns and large expenses
- **Too long (full year):** Accuracy degrades; assumptions become unrealistic
- **13 weeks (3 months):** Granular enough for weekly actions, far enough to catch upcoming challenges

**How it works:**
Every week, you drop the oldest week and add a new week 13 weeks out. This keeps you constantly looking ahead while staying grounded in recent reality.

## Building Your First Cash Flow Forecast: Step-by-Step

### Step 1: Choose Your Unit and Frequency

**Decision point:** Are you forecasting weekly, biweekly, or monthly?

- **Weekly:** Best for early-stage startups with high cash volatility
- **Monthly:** Standard for more mature startups ($1M+ ARR)
- **Biweekly:** Middle ground if you have moderate cash predictability

For this guide, we'll use **weekly** as the baseline, most common in Series A startups.

### Step 2: Map Your Revenue Streams

List every way cash enters your business:

| Revenue Source | Typical Timing | Timing Variability |
|---|---|---|
| Subscriptions (SaaS) | Upfront or monthly | Low - highly predictable |
| Project Services | End of month/after invoice | High - depends on client payment terms |
| Enterprise Contracts | Quarterly or upfront | Medium - large amounts, lumpy |
| Invoiced B2B Sales | 30-60 days post-invoice | High - depends on customer payment terms |
| Grants/VC Funding | When wire transfers | Very High - depends on external events |

**For each stream, document:**
- Expected monthly/weekly revenue
- Payment timing (invoice date vs. cash receipt)
- Payment terms (30-day net, upfront, etc.)
- Percentage you realistically collect (avoid assuming 100%)

### Step 3: Map Your Cash Outflows

Create a detailed expense calendar:

**Fixed (same every period):**
- Salaries and payroll taxes
- Rent
- Insurance
- Subscription tools

**Variable (fluctuates with activity):**
- Customer support (scales with customers)
- Hosting/infrastructure (scales with usage)
- Sales commissions (scales with revenue)
- Vendor costs

**Irregular (lumpy but predictable):**
- Quarterly tax payments
- Annual software licenses
- Equipment purchases
- Legal/compliance reviews

**Action item:** For each expense, note:
- Amount
- Who approves it
- Whether it's discretionary (can be delayed) or non-negotiable (must pay)

### Step 4: Create Your Excel Model

A simple 13-week rolling forecast has this structure:

```
Week 1    Week 2    Week 3    ...    Week 13
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Revenue
  Subscriptions     $8,500    $9,200    $9,200
  Services         $15,000   $12,000       $0
  Other                 $0        $0        $0
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Total Inflows     $23,500   $21,200    $9,200

Outflows
  Payroll          $18,000   $18,000   $18,000
  Hosting           $2,500    $2,500    $2,500
  Marketing         $5,000   $10,000    $5,000
  [Additional rows for all expenses]
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Total Outflows    $25,500   $30,500   $25,500

Beginning Cash     $45,000   $43,000   $33,700
Net Cash Flow      ($2,000)  ($9,300)  ($16,300)
Ending Cash        $43,000   $33,700   $17,400
```

### Step 5: Update Weekly

Pick a day each week (Monday is common) when you update the forecast:

1. **Adjust assumptions** - Did a customer delay payment? Update revenue timing.
2. **Add new expenses** - New hire approved? A tool subscription added?
3. **Review the 13-week horizon** - What's the earliest cash gap? When do you need funding?
4. **Drop oldest week, add week 14** - Keep it rolling forward.

## Critical Metrics to Track

### 1. Cash Runway

**Definition:** Months of cash remaining at current burn rate

**Formula:** 
```
Runway (months) = Current Cash / Average Monthly Burn
```

**Interpretation:**
- 0-3 months: Emergency funding needed (NOW)
- 3-6 months: Start fundraising conversation immediately
- 6-12 months: In a comfortable position to raise strategically
- 12+ months: Can focus on operations/growth

### 2. Cash Conversion Cycle

**Definition:** Days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers

**Formula:**
```
DaysInventoryOutstanding + DaysReceivableOutstanding - DaysPayableOutstanding
```

**Impact:** A 45-day cycle means your business must carry 45 days of cash to cover operations. Large enterprise customers (60-day terms) + fast supplier payments (14-day terms) = 46-day gap you must fund.

**For Iter Advisors' clients:** We often help optimize this cycle through better payment term negotiation and automation.

### 3. Monthly Cash Burn/Runway

Track this obsessively in your first 2 years:

| Month | Revenue | Expenses | Net Burn | Cumulative Burn | Runway (months) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | $30k | $45k | -$15k | -$15k | 10 |
| Feb | $35k | $48k | -$13k | -$28k | 9.2 |
| Mar | $50k | $50k | $0 | -$28k | infinite |

When net burn reaches zero, you've hit cash flow breakeven—a major milestone.

## Common Cash Flow Mistakes to Avoid

### Mistake 1: Being Too Optimistic on Revenue Timing

**Reality:** Enterprise deals close in Q4, then you wait 60+ days for payment. Your January cash suffers.

**Fix:** Use historical data, not best-case assumptions. If 30% of customers pay late, factor that in.

### Mistake 2: Forgetting Payroll Tax Liability

You withhold taxes from paychecks but owe the government monthly. If you spend withheld money on operating expenses, you create a liability bomb.

**Fix:** Segregate payroll tax in a separate account. Treat it as non-discretionary spend.

### Mistake 3: Underestimating "Nice to Have" Expenses

New tool subscriptions, tools, marketing experiments, team events, and professional development add up fast—often $2k-5k/month across dozens of small decisions.

**Fix:** Create a "discretionary expenses" bucket in your forecast. Track it separately so you know which items you can pause if cash tightens.

### Mistake 4: Forecasting Funding Like Guaranteed Income

"We're fundraising, so I'll assume Series A closes in Month 7" is dangerous.

**Fix:** Separate your "base case" forecast (no new funding) from your "optimistic" forecast. Know your actual runway without assuming new capital.

## FAQ: Cash Flow Forecasting

### Q: How frequently should we update our forecast?

**A:** Weekly is the gold standard for early startups (pre-Series B). Monthly works if you have predictable revenue and stable expenses. Daily updates are usually wasteful—your assumptions change slower than daily data.

### Q: What if revenue is too unpredictable to forecast?

**A:** Even high-volatility businesses (professional services, consulting, events) can forecast conservatively:
1. Use 50% of pipeline as "likely" (instead of 100%)
2. Push all revenue 1-2 weeks later than expected (to account for delays)
3. Track actual vs. forecast every week to improve your estimates over time

### Q: When should we raise money?

**A:** When your forecast shows 6 months of runway remaining. This gives you time to fundraise (3-4 months typical) without panic.

### Q: Should we include buffer for unexpected costs?

**A:** Yes. Add a "contingency" line item of 10-15% of operating expenses. Real emergencies happen (broken infrastructure, urgent hire, legal issue).

## Cash Flow Forecasting for Series A Companies: Special Considerations

If you're in or approaching Series A:

1. **Validate your LTV:CAC ratio** - Investors want to see that you're becoming more cash-efficient as you scale
2. **Model hiring plans** - Forecast the impact of planned team expansions on burn rate
3. **Include CapEx** - Major infrastructure investments should show up in the forecast
4. **Plan for loss period** - Growth stage often means negative unit economics initially; show when you'll turn to positive cash generation

Investors will ask for this forecast. Having it signals financial maturity.

## Conclusion: Cash Flow Forecasting is Your Strategic Tool

A 13-week rolling cash flow forecast does more than predict numbers—it forces you to think rigorously about:

- **When** cash actually comes in (not when you invoice)
- **Which** expenses are truly non-negotiable
- **How long** your runway actually is
- **Where** the biggest risks hide

Start this week. Create the basic model in Sheets. Update it every Monday. Share it with your co-founder and advisor.

Your future cash won't surprise you.
